CDC Workshop:
RobustControl@50 and Glover@80

Remembering Gunter Stein (1941-2026), Andy Packard (1960-2019), Gary Balas (1960-2014), Bruce Frances (1947-2018), and Allen Tannenbaum (1953-2023)

December 2026 — Hawaii

Workshop Materials

This will be the official website for the workshop. In the next few weeks, we will start posting background videos, slides, and curated reading lists. The workshop can be attended live without this background but given the limited time in one day it will help to have deeper background around the origins of robust control circa 1976, the precursors in classical and modern control, and background for the current challenges of robust AI and robustness of our large-scale infrastructure, including the environment and climate change. The workshop can also focus more on interactions with the audience. You won't have to attend the workshop to access the material here.

Additional organizers (and speakers) (with decade they started in robust control): Keith Glover (70s), John Doyle (70s), Pramod Khargonekar (80s), Anders Rantzer (90s), Mustafa Khammash (90s), Peter Seiler (00s), Antonis Papachristodoulou (00s), Dennice Gayme (00s), Li Na (10s), James Anderson (10s), Nik Matni (10s), Carmen Amo Alonso (20s).

Gunter Stein unfortunately died this year. We will highlight his contributions along with Packard, Balas, Francis, and Tannenbaum whose contributions were foundational but were gone too soon.

History

George Zames' 1976 CDC Plenary is widely viewed as the origin of what we now call Robust Control Theory (RCT). He critiqued LQG (H2) and proposed Hinf as a better alternative. Between 1976 and 1984, Zames published 12 papers on Hinf and robust control, 5 co-authored with Bruce Francis, clarifying and elaborating on the technical details of the 1976 vision. Subsequent RCT research focused on generalizations of Hinf and scalability of algorithms.

1976 was also the year Glover moved to Cambridge and introduced AAK theory in model reduction and later control. Doyle found "there are none" numerically in his 1976 BS thesis at MIT, then spent the summer working with Stein at Honeywell. Stein also began 1/4 time as an adjunct professor at MIT. In 1981 NATO organized a lecture tour of Europe on Multivariable Control, featuring Zames, Stein, and Doyle for RCT and MacFarlane, Willems, and Wonham for modern control methods.

1984 was another milestone in the growth of robust control, with the ONR/Honeywell Workshop, organized by Doyle, Stein, Francis, and Khargonekar, featuring Glover as well as Zames as the banquet keynote speaker. This underscored how Robust Control went from fringe to mainstream. By 1984, Glover, Doyle, and others had found solutions to the general Hinf problem, and the workshop focused on the remaining computational challenges of two "4 block" problems: in Hinf and mu (Structure Singular Values). The former was "solved" (to the extent LQG was) in the "DGKF" paper from 1989, and increasingly scalable algorithms were developed for mu, led by Packard.

1984 was also the first flight of the X-29 and the exciting story of the role of RCT can now be told. Applications beyond aerospace grew to chemical process control and energy systems in the 80s, and to internet and systems biology in the late 90s. A major advance in the 1990s was the Integration of Dynamic Nonlinear Inversion with mu-synthesis, though it's most dramatic application were still in aerospace.

Important historical comparisons included "classical control" from the early 1900 by engineers working on feedback amplifiers in the analog phone system, and led by Black, Bode, and Nyquist. Another is the fraught history of prediction, planning, and feedback in modern control and as far back as Copernicus. The promise and risks of AI methods make these successes and errors seem minor.

We will review this history in detail in online videos, slides, and papers, but focus the live workshop on a briefer summary and questions from attendees. But the primary focus will be on the lessons to be learned about robustness and complexity from this history and further developments in the 2000s. The new challenges from AI, robotics, automation, and CPS infrastructure need both robust control and robust architectures. Systems biology, medicine, and the myriad environmental threats are perhaps less sensational and but are equally important and challenging.

Major extensions (~2000) to nonlinear systems beyond NDI were based on Sum-of-Squares (SOS), which will be reviewed here and at CDC. Equally exciting developments in RCT have been subsequent extensions to distributed control in complex networks, and particularly Layering as Optimization (LaO) and Systems Level Synthesis (SLS). These will be explored online but minimized at CDC as there are a variety of other invited, tutorial, and regular sessions, particularly on SLS.

Another focus here online but less live is on the interplay between robust control and physics, from the battle over the ubiquity of power laws to collaborations on control systems for LIGO and CERN. Another is how recent discoveries of dramatic, systematic errors in climate modeling underscore how ignoring robustness in climate predictions can make them, unsurprisingly, overly optimistic. This is one of the biggest science stories of 2026 and will be reviewed here in some detail, with opportunities for discussion at CDC.
