History

George Zames' 1976 CDC Plenary is widely viewed as the origin of what we now call Robust Control Theory (RCT). He critiqued LQG (H2) and proposed Hinf as a better alternative. Between 1976 and 1984, Zames published 12 papers on Hinf and robust control, 5 co-authored with Bruce Francis, clarifying and elaborating on the technical details of the 1976 vision. Subsequent RCT research focused on generalizations of Hinf and scalability of algorithms.

1976 was also the year Glover moved to Cambridge and introduced AAK theory in model reduction and later control. Doyle found "there are none" numerically in his 1976 BS thesis at MIT, then spent the summer working with Stein at Honeywell. Stein also began 1/4 time in 1977 as an adjunct professor at MIT, which quickly shifted to being a leading academic center for Robust Control. In 1981 NATO organized a lecture tour of Europe on Multivariable Control, featuring Zames, Stein, and Doyle for RCT and MacFarlane, Willems, and Wonham for modern control methods.

1984 was another milestone in the growth of robust control, with the ONR/Honeywell Workshop, organized by Doyle, Stein, Francis, and Khargonekar, featuring Glover as well as Zames as the banquet keynote speaker. This underscored how Robust Control went from fringe to mainstream. By 1984, Glover, Doyle, and others had found solutions to the general Hinf problem, and the workshop focused on the remaining computational challenges of two "4 block" problems: in Hinf and mu (Structure Singular Values). The former was "solved" (to the extent LQG was) in the "DGKF" paper from 1989, and increasingly scalable algorithms were developed for mu, led by Packard.

1984 was also the first flight of the X-29 and the exciting story of the role of Honeywell and RCT in design of the flight control system can now be told. Applications beyond aerospace grew to chemical process control and energy systems in the 80s, and to internet and systems biology in the late 90s. A major advance in the 1990s was the Integration of Dynamic Nonlinear Inversion with mu-synthesis, though it's most dramatic application were still in aerospace.

Important historical comparisons included "classical control" from the early 1900 by engineers working on feedback amplifiers in the analog phone system, and led by Black, Bode, and Nyquist. Another is the fraught history of prediction, planning, and feedback in modern control and as far back as Copernicus. The promise and risks of AI methods make these successes and errors seem minor.

We will review this history in detail in online videos, slides, and papers, but focus the live workshop on a briefer summary and questions from attendees. The primary focus will be on the lessons to be learned about robustness and complexity from this history and further developments in the 2000s. The new challenges from AI, robotics, automation, and CPS infrastructure need both robust control and robust architectures. Systems biology, medicine, and the myriad environmental threats are perhaps less sensational and but are equally important and challenging.

Major extensions (~2000) to nonlinear systems beyond NDI were based on Sum-of-Squares (SOS), which will be reviewed here and at CDC. Equally exciting developments in RCT have been subsequent extensions to distributed control in complex networks, and particularly Layering as Optimization (LaO) and Systems Level Synthesis (SLS). These will be explored online but minimized at CDC as there are a variety of other invited, tutorial, and regular sessions, particularly on SLS.

Another focus here online but less live is on the interplay between robust control and physics, from the battle over the ubiquity of power laws to collaborations on control systems for LIGO and CERN. Another is how recent discoveries of dramatic, systematic errors in climate modeling underscore how ignoring robustness in climate predictions can make them, unsurprisingly, overly optimistic. This is one of the biggest science stories of 2026 and will be reviewed here in some detail, with opportunities for some discussion at CDC.

Respect Gunter Stein

By John Doyle and The Unstable

Gunter Stein was by far my most important influence in the field of controls, and arguably the central motivator in the origins of robust control. While he was a legend, many important stories about him have not been told due to proprietary, clearance, and corporate constraints, which are now effectively gone. Reality is much greater than legend, and this is a very personal account of Gunter and the origins and evolution of robust control.

We met in 1976 at MIT where I was an undergrad, and he was arranging an adjunct position where he then commuted from Minneapolis for the next 20 years. He easily persuaded me to spend the summer at Honeywell System and Research Center (HSRC) in Minneapolis where I was then a consultant. Through my PhD and my first year (1985) at Caltech, my main coauthor by far was Gunter [2], and all my coauthors were associated with HSRC. While robust control theory was even more heavily mathematical than its modern precursor, Gunter made sure it stayed relevant and that the "theory-practice gap" disappeared as an excuse for bad theory.

In my 1976 MIT BS thesis I found numerically that, for the widely conjectured LQG guaranteed margins, "there are none". Gunter found this convincing but correctly insisted that for skeptics, a Fortran card deck and pages of printer output would not be persuasive, and there was no accessible alternative. These were "dark ages" in many respects. In summer 1977 I found a simple analytic example requiring only high school algebra and submitted a one-page paper to IEEE TAC. Gunter was metaphorically a robust controller in the resulting adversarial editorial process with unstable plants and worst-case disturbances, and it was eventually published [3]. He also helped turn MIT into the early US academic hub of robust control, though the US (outside of Honeywell) initially started behind Canada, Europe, and Japan.

One of the most important projects in the early history of robust control was the flight control system for the X-29 (Figure 2). Gunter highlighted the X-29 in the first Bode Lecture [1] but could still only tell part of the story, and not the full impact of his contribution. In 1994 NASA released a report on the X-29 10 years after the first flight that extensively showed how HSRC's μ-analysis (Structured Singular Value) results on Grumman's models accurately predicted the real flight test data [4] What was not explained was how a few hacks using LQG could produce such a robust controller, which was utterly implausible.

The X-29 contract was awarded in late 1981 with Honeywell supplying the avionics and Digital Flight Control Computers (DFCC). The X-29 was revolutionary in many respects. The obvious cutting-edge feature was the forward swept wings that caused instability in both the wing itself and in the pitch control for the full aircraft. Grumman's composite material innovations mitigated the first, but their plan to use LQG for the latter was a looming disaster. In 1983 NASA awarded an additional contract to HSRC for an "independent audit" of the LQG DFC which Gunter leveraged to do a complete redesign.

In previous Grumman vehicles such as the F-14 (and others such as Apollo), modern control methods were used successfully for guidance and navigation, but the low-layer feedback controllers were designed with methods that used state space simulations, but were heavily classical, loop-at-a-time, and frequency domain. By 1978 we had published counterexamples to not just LQG, but all these standard methods and their modern extensions. NASA experts knew this and were naturally concerned.

Fortunately, by the 1983 "audit" we could do rigorous analysis using the new μ-analysis (Structured Singular Values) as highlighted in the 1994 NASA report. Unreported apparently anywhere was the role of μ-synthesis which was evolving along with progress in H∞ methods. But by 1983 μ-synthesis software produced only ugly high dimensional controllers which at the time were hard to reduce and implement on the DFCC. Using H2 (LQG) to approximate H∞ and thus μ-syn controllers was both a technical and political expedient. Technically, the LQG approximation was simple enough that it allowed Grumman to work with HSRC to implement it on the DFCC. Politically, Grumman could then "evolve" their LQG controllers from fragile to robust during the "audit," with no public mention of μ-synthesis. Beyond engineering, Gunter was again the stabilizing influence in what could have been a highly adversarial project. Without him, the project would likely have been a disaster.

Another landmark project for Gunter and robust control was the F-35. As a taxpayer, the F-35 was an abomination, but as a controls challenge it was unprecedented since gain-scheduling even robust controllers would be unacceptably fragile. While the HSRC design team led by Enns (with Balas@UMinnesota) used μ-synthesis methods throughout, they were heavily augmented by Nonlinear Dynamic Inversion (NDI) which was a full envelope upgrade to standard gain scheduling [5,6]. NDI/μ was developed in the 1990s and first flight-tested on the X-35 in 2000, with the first production F-35 flight in 2006. The NDI/μ approach has greatly expanded elsewhere since, with Incremental NDI (INDI) variants blending nonlinear, robust, and adaptive methods.

This nearly instant transition of NDI/μ from theory to practice was unprecedented and would have been impossible without Gunter's leadership and standing. An additional challenge in the 1990s was a series of mergers and acquisitions involving Honeywell that had a largely negative impact on research, and Gunter retired from both Honeywell and MIT in the late 90s. Much has been lost since in the aerospace industry, where crashes that would have been unheard of in the 90s have become too common. But robust control and its network extensions have become even more important in control of chemical process, power, internet, and robotics, and recently in the new challenge of robust ("safe") AI.

In retrospect, what stands out to me about Gunter was not merely his unmatched technical excellence, but his perfect blend of rigor, relevance, kindness, and toughness. Control and Dynamical Systems (CDS@Caltech) was started in 1994, and Gunter's smashing of the "theory-practice gap" was a central inspiration, as was his rejection of the bullying that epitomized modern control. His historic Bode lecture and paper permanently boosted interest in and broader applications of Bode-like conservation "laws." A striking example was the later application of these "laws" to finally explain previously cryptic oscillations in yeast metabolism as an inevitable universal trade-off between robustness and efficiency [7]. Gunter showed us how the right mathematics could illuminate both engineered flight and living nature.

Munther A Dahleh

I first met Gunter during my interview at MIT. Gunter was an adjunct professor in EECS and would visit LIDS for a couple of days every month. He immediately struck me as a brilliant engineer! As a naive researcher who was more of an applied mathematician than a real engineer, I was struck by how Gunter could see through the mathematics and distill what was truly essential for applications.

I had the luxury of interacting with Gunter over the decade that followed. In addition to the various conversations we had about the field, he also served on the thesis committees of my students and helped shape the scope and utility of their work. I personally learned an immense amount from him. His inaugural Bode Lecture [1] solidified my view of his remarkable ability to understand very different applications from a common fundamental perspective. In his talk "Respect the Unstable," he showed how the challenges posed by complex control engineering systems, from agile aircraft to the Chernobyl disaster, could be understood through the same fundamental ideas that explain the difficulty of controlling an inverted pendulum. Thirty years later, his lecture remains one of the most cited Bode Lectures and one that has had a clear impact on our community.

From my perspective, Gunter's contribution to the control field centered on the adoption of robust control methods in engineering design. Through his work at Honeywell and his collaborations with students and faculty at MIT and other universities, robust control entered virtually every area of control applications. He shaped the direction of our field for decades, and his ideas remain a driving force behind many of today's control applications.

On a personal level, Gunter was incredibly humble and unassuming, and always ready to offer help and support whenever asked. He was a giant in our field and played a major role in shaping it. I am deeply grateful for the opportunity to have learned from him and to have had him as a colleague. He will be dearly missed.
